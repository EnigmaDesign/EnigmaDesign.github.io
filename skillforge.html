The Problem

Great coaching exists. Almost no one can access it.

The e-sports coaching market is broken in two distinct ways — one about quality, one about cost. Both affect the same players.

🎮

Being good doesn't mean you can teach

Most coaches enter the market simply because they're skilled players — not because they understand pedagogy. There's no qualification filter. A Challenger-rank player and a Gold-rank player charge the same rates, and often deliver the same unclear advice.

No quality standard in the market
💸

€150–250/hour is not a market, it's a barrier

1:1 coaching sessions cost up to €250 per hour. One session won't make you better. A real improvement arc takes months of consistent practice and feedback. At those prices, only a tiny minority can afford the volume of sessions that actually moves the needle.

64% of users priced out
Problem Validation

350 players surveyed. 25 interviews. One clear conclusion.

Before any design or engineering work started, I ran a structured research phase to validate whether the problem was real, who it affected, and whether the proposed model could address it. The research is what drove every subsequent decision — including the decision to halt.

📊

The Solution Model

Pro knowledge. Scalable price.

Instead of 1:1 live sessions, SkillForge flips the model: pro players record structured educational content once, and players buy it at a fraction of the coaching cost.

01

Pro Players Record Content

Retired or active professionals record game-specific educational videos — explaining decision-making, mechanics, and strategy with the depth only a top-level player can provide.

02

Content Lives on the Platform

Videos are structured as courses and hosted on SkillForge — organized by title (LoL, Valorant, Fortnite, R6), role, skill level, and topic for precise discoverability.

03

Players Pay Per Course

Users buy the content they need at accessible prices. SkillForge charges a commission on each sale — keeping the platform sustainable while the pro player keeps the majority of the revenue.

04

Everyone Wins (in theory)

Pro players monetize their knowledge without time commitment. Players access elite-level content affordably. The platform grows with a scalable, content-first inventory model.

The Trade-off Analysis

The hardest part was the honest math.

The model worked on paper. The problem was in the execution. Here's the full picture of what we weighed before making the call.

What worked

Validated demand. Research across 4 titles confirmed players wanted affordable, high-quality improvement content.

4× TAM expansion. Expanding beyond LoL to Valorant, Fortnite, and R6 made the total addressable market significantly larger.

Scalable model. Unlike 1:1 coaching, recorded content scales — one video can serve thousands of buyers with zero marginal cost.

Margin improvement. Targeting retired pros instead of active players projected a 20% improvement in acquisition cost per creator.

What broke it

Creator acquisition is hard. Reaching top-tier pro players — even retired ones — requires industry connections, agent relationships, and significant upfront negotiation.

Cold-start content problem. A platform with no content has no users. A platform with no users attracts no creators. Breaking the loop requires capital or leverage we didn't have.

Quality control at scale. Ensuring educational quality across creators, titles, and roles without a structured vetting process was an unsolved operational problem.

Trust requires authority. Players pay for coaching when they trust the coach's reputation. Building that trust at platform-level takes time and budget we couldn't justify yet.

The Decision

Knowing when not to build.

We stopped. On purpose.

After validating the problem and stress-testing the business model, the assessment was clear: the bottleneck was creator acquisition — and solving it would require resources, industry access, and a go-to-market motion that wasn't realistic at this stage. Building a product that depends on supply you can't reliably generate isn't a risk worth taking.

This wasn't a failure of the idea. The market gap is real. The demand is there. The model is sound. But product discipline means recognizing the gap between a good opportunity and a buildable one — and making the call before sinking engineering and design time into something the supply side couldn't support.

That decision, and the structured thinking behind it, is what SkillForge actually demonstrates.
